 >>> COSMIC INTERPRETATION <<<

Today the sky performs a single operation: the cut. It is Kalashtami — the aṣṭamī of Kāla-Bhairava, Rudra's fierce form who severs what time will sever anyway, only faster and by choice rather than by force. The Moon rides through Ardrā, the nakṣatra ruled by Rudra himself — the storm that clears, the lightning that splits the tree. The yoga is Parigha, literally "the iron bar": the day-signature of obstruction and hard boundaries, demanding exactly the dṛḍha — the firmness — that BG 15.3 requires of the hand that wields the blade. Śanivāra adds Śani's discipline: the planet of limits and removal, who teaches through subtraction, never addition. Every marker of the day describes one verb: cutting.

Then, at 3:59 PM EDT, the operation completes its arc. The Moon crosses from Ardrā into Punarvasu — "the return of the light," ruled by Aditi, the boundless mother, whose deity-promise is that what was cut away was never the light itself. The evening opens toward Navami, and the yoga that arrives at 3:01 AM is Śiva — the auspicious. The sequence written across the day is the sequence of the verse: fierce morning severance (asaṅga-śastreṇa chittvā), followed by the afternoon's turn toward that from which having gone, none return (yasmin gatā na nivartanti). The waning Moon in Mithuna — the mind's own sign — diminishes toward Amāvāsyā as the verse demands the tree be cut down entirely. On Kalashtami, the darkness itself becomes the instrument.

 >>> VERSE SELECTION RATIONALE <<<

BG 15.3–4 Alignment: The chapter is Puruṣottama Yoga — the upside-down aśvattha tree of saṁsāra and the Person beyond it. Verses 15.1–2 describe the tree: roots above, branches below, nourished by the guṇas, rooting into karma. Verse 3 stops describing and starts operating: the tree's real form cannot be perceived, its end and beginning and foundation cannot be found — but it can be CUT. Today's panchāṅga is a one-day commentary on that operation. Kalashtami supplies the weapon's blessing: Kāla-Bhairava, who cuts the thread of time. Ardrā supplies the storm-quality of the cut: Rudra's lightning does not prune — it fells. Parigha yoga supplies the resistance: the iron bar is what the blade must meet, and meeting it is the practice. Śanivāra supplies the discipline to hold the grip firm (dṛḍhena). And Punarvasu's arrival at 3:59 PM supplies the verse's second movement: the search for the place of no-return, the return of the light that never actually left. The day is not merely matching the verse — it is demonstrating the verse's exact sequence, morning to night.

 >>> SĀṄKHYA LENS <<<

[The un-mappable system]: Sāṅkhya teaches that the material tree — prakṛti's full unfolding from mahat down to the elements — is one closed system of cause and effect. The puruṣa, consciousness, is NOT inside that system; it only appears to be, through the false identification (asmitā) that grafts it onto a branch. BG 15.3's claim that the tree's form "cannot be perceived" is thus precise metaphysics, not mysticism: from any position within prakṛti's network, the network's totality is unobservable, because every observer-position is a node of the network. Only what is not a node can see the whole. This is the first hint of why the verse turns immediately from perception to severance — the exit is not an insight you can have while inside; it is a cut you make.

[Attachment as load-bearing structure]: The tree of saṁsāra is sustained not by its wood but by saṅga — linkage, contact, investment. Each attachment is a claim: "this branch is me, this fruit is mine." Sāṅkhya's sequence of bondage runs exactly through this: saṅga feeds kāma, kāma feeds krodha, and the tree grows. asaṅga is therefore not one virtue among many — it is the single structural intervention that collapses the entire sequence. Cut the claim of ownership at any point and the branch starves; cut it at the root-inversion — the claim "I am the enjoyer" — and the whole tree falls. This is why the Gītā calls it a śastra, a weapon: it operates not on feelings but on structure.

[Terminal metaphor]: The tree is a legacy codebase with no identifiable entry point and no surviving documentation — every function you might call to inspect it is itself part of the codebase. asaṅga is not deleting files. It is revoking your own credentials as a user of the system. The code keeps running; you are simply no longer running as it. prapadye — the final commit — is handing the whole repository back to its original author.
