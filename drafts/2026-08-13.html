Bhagavad-gītā 11.8

na tu māṁ śakyase draṣṭum
anenaiva sva-cakṣuṣā
divyaṁ dadāmi te cakṣuḥ
paśya me yogam aiśvaram

na tu māṁ śakyase draṣṭum anenaiva sva-cakṣuṣā divyaṁ dadāmi te cakṣuḥ paśya me yogam aiśvaram

But you cannot see Me with your present eyes. Therefore I give you divine eyes. Behold My mystic opulence!

Cosmic Context — Panchāṅga Analysis (August 13, 2026, Philadelphia)

Tithi: Sukla Paksha Pratipada → Sukla Paksha Dwitiya (transition at 11:11 AM). Pratipada — the first day of the waxing moon, the new beginning after the void of Amāvasyā. Today is Chandra Darshan — the first sighting of the crescent moon after the new moon, a moment of hope and renewal. The transition to Dwitiya at 11:11 AM marks the deepening of the waxing phase.

Nakṣatra: Magha → Purva Phalguni (transition at 7:08 PM). Magha — the royal star, the throne of the Pitṛs (ancestors), authority and power. Its deity is the Pitṛs, its power is the ability to assume leadership and honor. The transition to Purva Phalguni — the former reddish one, the bhaga (prosperity) nakṣatra — marks the shift from ancestral authority to enjoyment and creativity.

Yoga: Parigha — the obstacle, the barrier. This yoga represents the obstruction that must be overcome — the limitation of ordinary vision. Yet Parigha also signifies the strength to break through barriers, the courage to face what blocks perception.

Karaṇa: Bava → Balava → Kaulava. Bava — the auspicious karana for material prosperity and family harmony. Balava — the karana of strength and courage, suitable for challenging endeavors. Kaulava — the karana of family and social connections, the bond of community.

Special Observance: Chandra Darshan — the first sighting of the moon after Amāvasyā. In the Vedic tradition, seeing the crescent moon is a sacred moment, a time to pray for fulfillment of desires and spiritual progress. It is the rekindling of light after darkness.

Verse Selection Rationale

The selection of BG 11.8 for this Chandra Darshan day is profoundly symbolic. Chandra Darshan — the first glimpse of the moon after Amāvasyā — represents the return of light, the renewal of perception. In BG 11.8, Kṛṣṇa tells Arjuna that he cannot see the Supreme with his present eyes, but promises to give him divine eyes to behold His mystic opulence. This is the exact parallel: the ordinary vision is insufficient; a higher vision is granted.

Pratipada tithi, the first day of the waxing moon, aligns with the promise of new perception — the beginning of a new cycle of seeing. Magha nakshatra, the royal throne, signifies the authority of the Supreme whose form is to be seen. Parigha yoga, the obstacle, mirrors the limitation of mundane vision that must be overcome by divine grace. Chandra Darshan, the first sighting of the moon, mirrors the granting of divine eyes to see the Lord’s form.

This verse is the threshold of perception in Chapter 11. Before this, Arjuna has desired to see the universal form; here, Kṛṣṇa conditions the vision on divine grace. After this, the universal form is revealed (verses 9-13). Thus BG 11.8 is the pivotal moment where the seeker is made fit to receive the cosmic vision — just as Chandra Darshan makes the devotee fit to perceive the renewed light.

Yoga Sūtra Connection: YS 1.20 — śraddhā-vīrya-smṛti-samādhi-prajñāpūrvaka itareṣām

YS 1.20 states: śraddhā-vīrya-smṛti-samādhi-prajñāpūrvaka itareṣām — “Others [attain samādhi] preceded by faith, energy, mindfulness, samādhi, and wisdom.”

The parallel to BG 11.8 is one of progressive qualification. Kṛṣṇa does not give divine eyes arbitrarily; He gives them to Arjuna because of Arjuna’s faith and desire. The Yoga Sūtras teach that higher states (like the divine vision of the Lord) are attained through the cultivation of faith (śraddhā), energy (vīrya), mindfulness (smṛti), samādhi, and wisdom (prajñā). Arjuna’s faith and devotion qualify him to receive this grace.

Alternatively, YS 1.23 — īśvara-praṇidhāna vā — “Or through devotion to the Lord.” This sutra states that samādhi can be attained through devotion to the Supreme. BG 11.8 is the direct result of such devotion: Arjuna’s desire to see the Lord’s form, rooted in his devotion, earns him the gift of divine eyes. The universal form is not seen by intellectual effort but by the grace that flows from devotion.

For the practitioner, the teaching is clear: the vision of the Divine — whether the cosmic form or the beautiful form — is not granted to the intellect alone. It requires the purification of the senses through devotion, faith, and inner strength. Just as the moon is first seen after Amāvasyā only when the sky is clear and the observer is attentive, the Divine is seen when the inner obstacles are removed by grace.

Practical Application for the Day

On this Chandra Darshan day, the practitioner stands at the threshold of perception. The old darkness of Amāvasyā has passed; the new light has not yet fully grown. The crescent moon is a sliver of light — a promise, not the fullness. Similarly, in spiritual practice, we often receive glimpses — not the full revelation, but enough to sustain hope and effort.

For the seeker: The limitation of “present eyes” (BG 11.8) is the reliance on ordinary perception, intellect, and ego-bound vision. The practice today is to recognize this limitation and sincerely pray for the gift of divine eyes — the vision that sees beyond the material. This is not a passive waiting; it is an active cultivation of faith and devotion, the qualities that make the soul receptive to grace.

For the practitioner: The transition from Magha to Purva Phalguni nakshatra is the transition from ancestral authority to enjoyment and creativity. Magha’s royal energy demands discipline and honor; Purva Phalguni’s energy invites the enjoyment of the fruits of that discipline. The practice today is to honor the authority of the Supreme (Magha) while allowing the joy of spiritual progress (Purva Phalguni) to arise. Parigha yoga reminds us that obstacles exist — the tendency to rely solely on logic, the fear of the unknown, the doubt that blocks faith. The practice is to confront these obstacles with courage (Balava karana) and trust that the Divine will provide the means to overcome them.

Chandra Darshan teaches us to value the first glimpse. Do not wait for the full moon to begin your practice. The sliver of light is enough to guide your steps. Similarly, in spiritual life, the first taste of divine vision — however small — is enough to strengthen faith and motivate further surrender. The practice today is to cherish whatever vision you have received, however modest, and to use it as a stepping stone toward greater clarity.

As BG Chapter 11 progresses, remember that the universal form (verses 9-13) is granted only after the qualification of verse 8. The vision is not a right; it is a gift. Honor the gift by using it to deepen devotion, not to feed ego. The ultimate goal is not the vision itself but the lover who sees — the devotee who, having seen the Lord’s opulences, falls ever more in love with His beautiful, two-armed form.

Sanskrit Terms

na tu māṁ śakyase draṣṭum (phrase) — “You cannot see Me with your present eyes”; the acknowledgment of the limitation of ordinary vision. Like a computer trying to run a program beyond its hardware capacity — an upgrade is needed.

divyaṁ dadāmi te cakṣuḥ (phrase) — “I give you divine eyes”; the granting of higher perception by grace. Not earned by effort alone, but given as a gift to the qualified devotee. The spiritual equivalent of receiving a firmware update that enables new capabilities.

paśya me yogam aiśvaram (phrase) — “Behold My mystic opulence!”; the invitation to see the Lord’s divine powers and manifestations. The opulence (aiśvarya) is not material wealth but the inconceivable powers of the Supreme.

śraddhā-vīrya-smṛti-samādhi-prajñāpūrvaka (Yoga Sūtra 1.20) — “preceded by faith, energy, mindfulness, samādhi, and wisdom”; the fivefold qualification for higher states. Each term represents a necessary inner development: faith as trust, energy as zealous effort, mindfulness as recollection of the goal, samādhi as focused absorption, wisdom as discriminative knowledge.

Chandra Darshan (noun) — the first sighting of the moon after the new moon; the moment when the crescent moon becomes visible in the western sky after sunset. A sacred time for prayer and renewal.

Summary

BG 11.8 is the threshold of perception in the cosmic vision — Kṛṣṇa tells Arjuna that ordinary eyes cannot see His universal form, but promises to give divine eyes to behold His mystic opulence. On this Chandra Darshan day, the day of first sighting the moon after Amāvasyā, this verse speaks directly to the practitioner’s experience: ordinary vision is insufficient; higher vision is granted by grace.

Just as the crescent moon appears after the void of Amāvasyā, the divine vision appears after the recognition of one’s perceptual limitations. Pratipada tithi signals the new beginning of perception; Magha nakshatra signals the royal authority of the One to be seen; Parigha yoga signals the obstacle to be overcome; Chandra Darshan signals the fulfillment of the promise.

As Chapter 11 continues, the universal form will be revealed (verses 9-13) — but only to those who have received the gift of divine eyes. The practice today is to cultivate the qualifications of faith, energy, mindfulness, samādhi, and wisdom, and to sincerely pray for the grace to see. Remember: the goal is not the vision itself, but the ever-deepening love for the Supreme that such vision inspires.
