Bhagavad Gita Chapter 13, Verse 14

Translation (Prabhupada):
Everywhere are His hands and legs, His eyes, heads and faces, and He has ears everywhere. In this way the Supersoul exists, pervading everything.

Commentary: The Omnipresence of the Divine

Today’s cosmic configuration features the convergence of Kalashtami and Krishna Janmashtami—two festivals that highlight the Divine’s omnipotence and intimate presence. Kalashtami, dedicated to Lord Bhairava, the fierce form of Shiva who destroys fear and time, emphasizes the Lord’s power over dissolution and terror. Krishna Janmashtami celebrates the birth of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, whose divine form pervades all creation. The planetary energies reinforce this theme: the Moon in Taurus (steadiness, nourishment) and Sun in Leo (royalty, vitality) reflect the Lord’s sustaining and majestic presence. The transitional Vyaghata yoga (obstacle-smashing) until mid-morning gives way to Harshana yoga (joy, prosperity), symbolizing the removal of obstacles to experiencing the Divine’s all-pervading grace.

BG 13.14 captures the essence of divine omnipresence: the Supersoul’s hands, legs, eyes, heads, faces, and ears exist everywhere, and He pervades all living beings by covering them with His presence. This is not a metaphorical statement but a literal truth of the Spiritual Reality: the Supreme Personality of Godhead, though residing in His transcendental abode, extends His potency to every atom of creation. The living entity, conditioned by material nature, possesses limited senses and a localized body, but the Supersoul transcends all limitations. His senses are not material yet are the source of all sensory activities in the universe. He is unattached yet the maintainer of all. This verse invites the practitioner to meditate on the all-pervading nature of the Lord, thereby dissolving the false notion of separation between the self and the Divine.

From a Sankhya perspective, the Supersoul is the transcendental puruṣa that permeates prakṛti without being contaminated by it. Like the all-pervading ether (ākāśa) that does not mix with elements yet provides the medium for sound, the Supersoul remains distinct while sustaining the material world. The practitioner’s buddhi (discriminative intelligence), when directed toward the Supersoul, recognizes that all activities occur within the field of prakṛti, yet the self remains the witness—similar to how the sun illuminates the universe without being entangled in its phenomena. This realization cultivates vairāgya (dispassion) toward material identifications and bhakti (devotion) toward the omnipresent Lord, whose presence is felt in every breath, every thought, and every action.

Yoga Sutra Connection:

BG 13.14’s description of the Supersoul’s all-pervading nature finds its parallel in Yoga Sutra 1.23: īśvara-praṇidhāna — devotion to the Lord. When one fixes the mind on the Supreme Lord through īśvara-praṇidhāna, the mind becomes steady, and the obstacles to samādhi begin to dissolve. This steady devotion allows the practitioner to perceive the Lord’s omnipresence—not as a philosophical concept but as a direct experiential reality. The cessation of mental fluctuations (citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ, YS 1.2) that follows is not a technique but the natural result of fixing attention on the All-Pervading One, whose nature is to pervade everything without limitation.

From a Sankhya viewpoint, īśvara-praṇidhāna represents the alignment of buddhi with puruṣa (the Supreme Lord). When buddhi is thus engaged, it performs its proper function of discriminating between the transient modifications of prakṛti and the eternal nature of puruṣa. This discriminative faculty, honed through devotion, allows one to see that the Supersoul is the original source of all senses yet remains unattached, the maintainer of all yet untouched by material qualities. Thus the practice of devotion leads to the direct realization expressed in BG 13.14: the Lord’s hands, legs, eyes, and ears are everywhere, and He pervades all existence.
