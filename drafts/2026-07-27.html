Bhagavad Gita Chapter 10, Verse 29

अनन्तश्चास्मि नागानां वरुणो यादसामहम् । पितॄणामर्यमा चास्मि यम: संयमतामहम् ॥ २९ ॥

 

anantaś cāsmi nāgānāṁ varuṇo yādasām aham pitṝṇām aryamā cāsmi yamaḥ saṁyamatām aham

 

Of the many-hooded Nāgas I am Ananta, and among the aquatics I am the demigod Varuṇa. Of departed ancestors I am Aryamā, and among the dispensers of law I am Yama, the lord of death.

 

Today is Śukla Caturdaśī (14th lunar day of the waxing moon) — the penultimate tithi before Pūrṇimā (full moon). The nakṣatra is Pūrva Aṣāḍā (until 3:41 AM tomorrow), ruled by Apa — the cosmic Waters. This is Somavāra (Monday), governed by the Moon and sacred to Śiva. The yoga transitions from Vaidhṛti (until 1:24 PM) to Viṣkambha — from "obstruction" to "support," mirroring the climbing energy toward full moon illumination. BG 10.29 positions Kṛṣṇa as the supreme among the cosmic administrators: Ananta among serpents, Varuṇa among aquatics, Aryamā among ancestors, Yama among lawgivers. Each of these beings governs a domain of cosmic order — and Kṛṣṇa declares Himself the essence of each. This is the Vibhūti Yoga teaching at its core: the Divine is not separate from the world's governing principles. He IS the supreme expression of each. Pūrva Aṣāḍā's ruling deity is Apa — the Waters. In Vedic cosmology, waters represent the cosmic ocean of potentiality, the medium through which all forms arise and dissolve. Varuṇa, who features in this verse, is the administrator of those waters — the guardian of ṛta (cosmic order). He sees everything, for the waters flow everywhere. Nothing is hidden from Varuṇa's gaze. This resonates with the nakṣatra's energy: Pūrva Aṣāḍā means "early victory" or "the unconquered" — the soul who rises through aligning with cosmic law rather than fighting against it. The purport emphasizes the hierarchy: Ananta is the greatest of the Nāgas, Varuṇa the greatest of aquatics, Aryamā the presiding ancestor, Yama the chief of law-keepers. They all represent Kṛṣṇa because they all administer His order. Yama, mentioned here as saṁyamatām aham — "of those who restrain, I am Yama" — enforces the consequences of action. Every soul faces this reckoning: the materialist after death, the yogī through discipline, the bhakta through surrender. The question is not whether you face Yama's mirror, but whether you face it awake. The sāṅkhya underpinning is precise: Prakṛti operates through fixed law (ṛta), not random chaos. The guṇas bind through measurable, predictable mechanics. Varuṇa administers the water element (ap), one of the five mahābhūtas. Yama administers death — the inevitable dissolution of each form back into its elements. Ananta supports the entire cosmos on his hoods — the substrate of physical reality. Aryamā tends the ancestors — the chain of karmic inheritance. Each function is a face of prakṛti's governance, and each is rooted in puruṣa — the Supreme Person, Kṛṣṇa. The Yoga Sutras connection is direct: YS 2.30-31 lists the five yamas — ahiṁsā, satya, asteya, brahmacarya, aparigraha — as the absolute, universal restraints. YS 2.32 lists the niyamas, including śauca (purification). Interestingly, Varuṇa's domain — water — is the primary medium of purification in Vedic culture. Śauca is both external (bathing, cleanliness) and internal (purifying the mind of kleshas). YS 2.1 defines kriyā-yoga as tapas-svādhyāya-īśvara-praṇidhāna — discipline, self-study, and devotion to God. This verse in the Gita reveals WHO that God is: the source of all cosmic law, the administrator of all restraint. When Patañjali says īśvara-praṇidhāna, the Gita answers: that Īśvara is Kṛṣṇa, who is Varuṇa among waters, Yama among restrainers, the very essence of cosmic order. Viṣkambha yoga (beginning 1:24 PM) means "supported" or "prop" — it is the energy of foundation, of being upheld. To surrender to Kṛṣṇa as the supreme administrator is to be viṣkambha — supported by the very structure of reality itself. The one who fights cosmic law struggles against the current. The one who aligns with it — who recognizes Varuṇa's waters, Yama's discipline, Ananta's patience — moves with the current toward the full moon of Liberation. Pūrva Aṣāḍā's "early victory" is not victory over the world, but victory over one's own resistance to divine order. It is the bhakta's insight: I do not conquer the world by force. I conquer my resistance to it. When Kṛṣṇa says He is Varuṇa among aquatics, He is telling us: the cosmic law that governs oceans and rivers also governs your subtle body. Purification is not optional — it IS the path. The waters of surrender wash away the samskaras that decades of karma have carved. As Caturdaśī builds toward Pūrṇimā, the moon's near-full light in Sagittarius (Dhanu rāśi — the Archer, the sign of dharma) calls us to aim higher. The lawgiver's gavel falls whether we acknowledge it or not. The bhakta's intelligence lies in recognizing the lawgiver as the Beloved — and bowing in love, not compulsion. Yama is not just death; Yama is restraint, niyama. Varuṇa is not just the sea; Varuṇa is the water that cleanses the mirror of the mind (ceto-darpaṇa-mārjanaṁ). Ananta is not just a serpent; Ananta is the infinite patience required for sustained practice. Today, align with cosmic law. Let Pūrva Aṣāḍā's waters carry what no longer serves you. Let Viṣkambha's support hold you as you climb toward fullness. And know that every force that governs the cosmos — from the depths of the ocean to the threshold of death — is a face of the One you serve.

Wherever you feel strain, ask: "Am I supporting the cosmos, or is the cosmos supporting me?" Let the answer reorganize your posture of practice.

 

TERM DEFINITIONS:

anantaḥ (noun) — Ananta ("the infinite"), the serpent Śeṣa who supports the universe on his hoods; represents infinite patience and cosmic substrate

nāgānām (genitive plural) — of the Nāgas (many-hooded serpents); cosmic beings associated with the substrate of physical reality

varuṇaḥ (noun) — Varuṇa, the Vedic demigod who governs the waters and enforces cosmic order (ṛta); the all-seeing administrator of moral law

yādasām (genitive plural) — of the aquatics, of water-dwelling beings; all life forms residing in cosmic and earthly waters

pitṝṇām (genitive plural) — of the departed ancestors; the lineage of karmic inheritance across generations

aryamā (noun) — Aryamā, the Vedic deity presiding over the ancestors (Pitṛloka); represents the continuity of dharma through lineage

yamaḥ (noun) — Yama, the lord of death and dispenser of justice; the cosmic lawgiver who administers consequences of action (karma-vipāka)

saṁyamatām (genitive plural) — of those who restrain, of regulators; those who exercise self-control and enforce cosmic law

aham (pronoun) — I am; the Supreme Personality of Godhead declaring His identity with each cosmic administrator
