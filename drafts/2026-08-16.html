Bhagavad-gītā 11.15

arjuna uvāca
paśyāmi devāṁs tava deva dehe
sarvāṁs tathā bhūta-viśeṣa-saṅghān
brahmāṇam īśaṁ kamalāsana-stham
ṛṣīṁś ca sarvān uragāṁś ca divyān

arjuna uvāca paśyāmi devāṁs tava deva dehe sarvāṁs tathā bhūta-viśeṣa-saṅghān brahmāṇam īśaṁ kamalāsana-stham ṛṣīṁś ca sarvān uragāṁś ca divyān

Arjuna said: My dear Lord Kṛṣṇa, I see assembled in Your body all the demigods and various other living entities. I see Brahmā sitting on the lotus flower, as well as Lord Śiva and all the sages and divine serpents.

Cosmic Context — Panchāṅga Analysis (August 16, 2026, Philadelphia)

Tithi: Śukla Pakṣa Pañcamī (transition from Caturthī at 7:22 AM). Pañcamī — the fifth lunar day, sacred to Nāga worship. Today is Nāg Pañcamī, the festival honoring divine serpents as cosmic powers and protectors. What begins as reverence for the feared becomes recognition of the sacred serpent as divine ornament (uragāṁś ca divyān).

Nakṣatra: Hastā → Citrā (transition at 6:20 PM). Hastā — "the Hand" — ruled by Savitṛ, the divine illuminator, governs dexterity, skillful execution, and the power to grasp what is envisioned. Citrā — "the Bright" — ruled by Tvaṣṭṛ, the celestial craftsman, governs artistic creation, beautiful arrangement, and the power to adorn. The transition from skilled hand to divine craftsmanship mirrors Arjuna's vision: he sees not just forms, but the divine artistry that adorns them.

Yoga: Sādhya → Subha (transition at 6:37 PM). Sādhya — "that which is to be accomplished" — the energy of remaining work. Subha — "auspicious, fortunate" — the yoga of goodness, beauty, and divine grace. Together they complete the arc: what remains to be done is done under divine grace, transforming effort into auspiciousness.

Vara: Raviwar (Sunday). Ravi — Sūrya, the Sun, the sovereign lord of light and dharma. Sunday illuminates the inner throne. The Sun’s rays reveal what was hidden in darkness, making the invisible visible — just as Kṛṣṇa revealed the universal form to Arjuna’s eyes.

Special Configuration: Simha Saṁkrānti (Sun enters Leo, the royal sign) + Nāg Pañcamī (divine serpents honored) + Hastā (the skilled hand) + Subha Yoga (auspicious grace) = the cosmic vision of the Lord as both supreme sovereign (Siṁha) and serpent-ornamented divine (uragāṁś ca divyān). The King wears the serpent as His adornment — fear transmuted into divine glory.

Verse Selection Rationale

Today’s panchāṅga demands a verse that honors Nāg Pañcamī while acknowledging Simha Saṁkrānti and the week’s transition into auspicious grace. BG 11.15 is the precise answer: Arjuna’s declaration, “I see [...] ṛṣīṁś ca sarvān uragāṁś ca divyān” — “I see all the sages and divine serpents.” On Nāg Pañcamī, when serpents are worshipped as cosmic powers, the Lord reveals them not as objects of fear but as divine adornments within His universal form.

Simha Saṁkrānti (Sun entering Leo) adds the royal dimension — the Lord as sovereign lion-king — yet His throne is adorned with serpents, just as Śiva wears Vāsuki as His garland. Hastā nakṣatra (“the Hand”) reminds us that vision requires the instrument to receive: Arjuna’s eyes, divinely empowered, are the hand that grasps what the mind cannot fabricate. Subha yoga crowns the vision with auspiciousness — seeing serpents as divine is not terror but śubha, fortunate grace.

This verse avoids the terror of later chapters (11.25ff — the terrible teeth, the mouths of death) and instead focuses on the beatific vision: the Lord as the container of all gods, sages, and sacred serpents. It is the darśana before the dread — the mercy that precedes the lesson that all warriors march into His mouths. Today, we begin with the vision that transforms fear into worship.

Yoga Sūtra Connection: YS I.41 — samāpattiḥ

YS I.41 describes samāpatti: “When the mind is free from all their distractions [...] it becomes pure like a crystal, maṇi — a crystal reflects whatever object is placed adjacent to it.” Arjuna’s divine vision in BG 11.15 is the perfection of samāpatti: his mind, cleared of fear and distraction by Kṛṣṇa’s grace, becomes the transparent crystal that reflects the universal form exactly as it is.

The Sanskrit maṇi (jewel, crystal) is the key. A flawed mind distorts reflection; a purified mind (sattva, freed from rajas and tamas) becomes maṇavat — jewel-like — and reflects without alteration. Arjuna does not imagine the serpents; he sees them because his citta has become maṇi. This is not psychic power but the result of īśvara-praṇidhāna — devotion to the Lord — which purifies the mind to receive divine revelation.

YS II.45 confirms the source: samādhi-siddhir īśvara-praṇidhānāt — “From devotion to the Lord comes the perfection of samādhi.” Arjuna’s vision is not earned by technique alone; it is granted by Kṛṣṇa’s mercy in response to Arjuna’s surrender and īśvara-praṇidhāna throughout the Gītā’s dialogue. The serpent vision (uragāṁś ca divyān) is the fruit of this devotion: the mind purified becomes the mirror that shows the Lord’s adornments as they truly are — not terrifying, but divinely beautiful.

Thus, YS I.41 + II.45 form the complete arc: devotion purifies the mind (īṡvara-praṇidhāna), the purified mind becomes crystal (samāpatti), and the crystal mind reflects the universal form exactly — sages, gods, and divine serpents — as ornaments of the Lord’s glory, not causes of fear.

Practical Application for the Day

Nāg Pañcamī teaches that what we fear — serpents, death, the unknown — can be seen as divine when our vision is purified. Arjuna does not close his eyes at the sight of uragāṁś ca divyān; he sees them as part of the Lord’s glorious form. Today’s practice is to look at what frightens you and ask: if this were seen in the universal form, how would it appear as divine ornament?

Hastā nakṣatra (“the Hand”) calls for skilled reception, not passive seeing. Just as a jeweler’s hand must be steady to set a gem, the practitioner’s inner instrument must be steady to receive divine vision. Today, stabilize your inner instrument through īśvara-praṇidhāna — offer your actions to the Lord — so that your mind becomes maṇi, crystal-clear, ready to reflect what is truly there.

Subha yoga reminds us that vision brings auspiciousness when it is not colored by fear. When you see a situation that usually provokes anxiety, pause and offer it to the Lord: “Let me see this as You see it.” Not denial, not avoidance — but the request for divine vision that transforms uraga (serpent) into divya uraga (divine serpent), fear into worship.

Simha Saṁkrānti adds the royal confidence: the lion-king does not flee from serpents; He wears them as His glory. Today, face what frightens you with the quiet assurance that, in the Lord’s vision, it is already transmuted into adornment. Your task is not to eliminate the serpent but to see it as He sees it — uragāṁś ca divyān.

Sanskrit Terms

uragāṁś ca divyān (n.) — “and divine serpents”; uraga = serpent, divya = divine, luminous, shining with inner light. In BG 11.15, Arjuna sees serpents not as objects of fear but as divyān — illuminated by the Lord’s light, wearing their venom as ornament rather than threat. The ca (“and”) places them on equal footing with sages (ṛṣīṁś ca) and gods (devāṁs) — all are divine in the Lord’s body.

samāpatti (n.) — “complete absorption, identification”; from samāp (toward completion) + āpatti (attainment). In YS I.41, samāpatti is the state where the mind, free from vṛttis (distractions), becomes like a crystal (maṇi) that reflects whatever is placed before it without distortion. It is not imagination but precise reflection — the mind as mirror, not as projector.

maṇi (n.) — “jewel, crystal, gem”; that which reflects without altering. A maṇi does not add color; it shows the object as it is. In yoga psychology, the sattvic mind becomes maṇavat — jewel-like — capable of reflecting objects (including divine forms) exactly as they exist, uncolored by rajas (passion) or tamas (ignorance). Arjuna’s mind, purified by īśvara-praṇidhāna, becomes maṇi.

īśvara-praṇidhāna (n.) — “devotion to the Lord”; the offering of one’s actions, mind, and life to Īśvara as the supreme object of love and surrender. In YS II.45, īśvara-praṇidhāna is the direct cause of samādhi-siddhi (perfection of meditative absorption). It is not technique alone but the love that invites the Lord’s grace to purify the mind for divine vision.

simha (n.) — “lion”; the sovereign beast, symbol of royalty, fearlessness, and dharma. In Simha Saṁkrānti, the Sun enters Leo (Siṁha), marking the time when sovereign power ascends. The Lord as Siṁha-pati (Lord of Lions) wears the serpent not as threat but as ornament — uragāṁś ca divyān — showing that true sovereignty transmutes fear into glory.

Summary

BG 11.15 is Arjuna’s beatific vision: the universal form not as terror but as divine cosmos, complete with sages, gods, and sacred serpents. On Nāg Pañcamī, when serpents are worshipped, the Lord reveals them as uragāṁś ca divyān — divine serpents, His adornments, not His enemies. This is the mercy that precedes the lesson: before showing the mouths that devour warriors (11.25ff), Kṛṣṇa first shows the form that shelters all.

YS I.41 provides the instrument: samāpatti — the mind as maṇi (crystal) that reflects without distortion. Through īśvara-praṇidhāna (YS II.45), the mind is purified to become maṇavat, jewel-like, ready to receive the universal form exactly as it is. The serpents are not imagined; they are seen because the inner mirror is clear.

Today’s panchāṅga — Nāg Pañcamī, Hastā (the skilled hand), Subha (auspicious grace), Simha Saṁkrānti (solar royalty) — forms a mandala around one teaching: what you fear can be seen as divine when your vision is purified. The lion-king wears the serpent as His glory. The practitioner, with steady inner instrument, sees uragāṁś ca divyān and offers worship, not fear. This is the darśana that transforms: not the destruction of the serpent, but the transmutation of fear into worship through divine vision.
