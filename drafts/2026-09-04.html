Bhagavad Gita Chapter 13, Verse 15

सर्वेन्द्रियगुणाभासं सर्वेन्द्रियविवर्जितम् । असक्तं सर्वभृच्चैव निर्गुणं गुणभोक्तृ च ॥ १५ ॥
sarvendriya-guṇābhāsaṁ
sarvendriya-vivarjitam
asaktaṁ sarva-bhṛt caiva
nirguṇaṁ guṇa-bhoktṛ ca
Translation (Prabhupada):
The Supersoul is the original source of all senses, yet He is without senses. He is unattached, although He is the maintainer of all living beings. He transcends the modes of nature, and at the same time He is the master of all the modes of material nature.

Commentary: The Transcendental Nature of the Divine

Today’s cosmic configuration marks the continuation of the Janmashtami celebrations, with Rohini nakshatra (the auspicious star under which Krishna appeared) still in influence until early afternoon. The tithi has moved from Ashtami to Navami, maintaining the sacred connection to Krishna’s divine appearance. The planetary energies reinforce this theme: Vajra yoga (indestructible, thunderbolt-like) until mid-morning gives way to Siddhi yoga (perfection, accomplishment), symbolizing the divine power that manifests in the material world. The Moon’s transit from Taurus (stability, nourishment) to Gemini (communication, versatility) reflects Krishna’s ability to sustain creation while engaging in divine pastimes. The Sun in Leo adds regal brilliance, reminding us of the Supreme Lord’s majestic opulence. This field invites recognition of the Lord’s transcendental nature—His divine senses are the source of all sensory activities yet remain uncontaminated by matter.

BG 13.15 reveals the Supersoul’s paradoxical nature: He is the original source of all senses (sarvendriya-guṇābhāsaṁ), yet possesses no material senses (sarvendriya-vivarjitam). Though unattached (asaktaṁ), He maintains all living beings (sarva-bhṛt). He transcends the three guṇas (nirguṇaṁ) while simultaneously being the master of their play (guṇa-bhoktṛ ca). This is not a philosophical abstraction but a literal truth of the Spiritual Reality: the Supreme Personality of Godhead, though residing in His transcendental abode, extends His potency to every atom of creation. His senses are not material yet are the source of all sensory activities in the universe. He remains detached yet is the sustainer of all. This verse reveals how the Lord can appear as a human child in Mathura’s prison cell while simultaneously maintaining the cosmic order—His divine senses operate on a transcendental plane, uncontaminated by material limitations.

From a Sankhya perspective, the Supersoul is the transcendental puruṣa that permeates prakṛti without being conditioned by it. Like the all-pervading ether (ākāśa) that enables sound without mixing with elements, the Supersoul remains distinct while sustaining the material world. The living entity, covered by material senses, experiences the world through guṇic modifications, but the Supersoul’s senses are nirguṇa—free from sattva, rajas, and tamas. His unattached nature (asaktaṁ) means He does not identify with the body or its designations, yet as sarva-bhṛt, He nourishes all beings. This realization cultivates vairāgya (dispassion) toward material identifications and bhakti (devotion) toward the transcendental Lord, whose presence is felt not through material senses but through the awakening of the soul’s innate spiritual senses. In the context of Janmashtami, this verse explains how Krishna, though appearing as a human infant, remains the unlimited Source of all senses, qualities, and opulences.

Yoga Sutra Connection:

BG 13.15’s description of the Supersoul’s transcendental senses finds its echo in Yoga Sutra 1.20: śraddhāvīryasmṛtisamādhipūrvaka itara — “Others [attain samādhi] through faith, energy, mindfulness, and meditative absorption.” This sutra describes the path to direct realization of the puruṣa through devotion and practice. The Supersoul’s transcendental senses (nirguṇa yet sarvendriya-guṇābhāsaṁ) are perceived not through material organs but through the purified buddhi engaged in devotion. When the practitioner cultivates śraddhā (faith) in the Lord’s transcendental nature, vīrya (energy) in devotional service, smṛti (mindfulness) of His divine qualities, and samādhi (absorption) in His presence, the material senses are transcended, allowing direct experience of the Lord’s spiritual senses. This aligns with the verse’s declaration that the Lord is without material senses yet the source of all senses—realization comes not through the eyes but through the eye of devotion.

From a Sankhya viewpoint, the Supersoul’s nirguṇa nature means His senses are not products of prakṛti’s guṇas. They are transcendental, belonging to the puruṣa tattva. The individual soul, conditioned by material senses, perceives the world through the guṇic lens—pleasure/pain (rajas/tamas), activity/rest, attraction/aversion. But when buddhi is purified through abhyāsa (practice) and vairāgya (detachment), and fixed on the Supersoul through īśvara-praṇidhāna (devotion to the Lord), it gains the capacity to perceive the Lord’s transcendental senses. This is not a suppression of the senses but their elevation: the material senses become instruments for perceiving the divine, just as a radio tuned to the right frequency can receive invisible waves. The result is a consciousness that recognizes the Lord as the source of all senses yet unattached to matter—a realization that dawns when the heart is fixed on Krishna through devotional practice, allowing the soul to experience the Supreme’s spiritual senses directly.
