Bhagavad-gītā 11.50

sañjaya uvāca
ity arjunaṁ vāsudevas tathoktvā
svakaṁ rūpaṁ darśayām āsa bhūyaḥ
āśvāsayām āsa ca bhītam enaṁ
bhūtvā punaḥ saumya-vapur mahātmā

sañjaya uvāca — Sañjaya said; ity arjunaṁ vāsudevaḥ tathā uktvā — having thus spoken to Arjuna, Vāsudeva; svakam rūpam darśayām āsa bhūyaḥ — showed His own form again; āśvāsayām āsa ca bhītam enam — and encouraged the fearful Arjuna; bhūtvā punaḥ — becoming again; saumya-vapuḥ — the beautiful form; mahā-ātmā — the great one.

Sañjaya said to Dhṛtarāṣṭra: The Supreme Personality of Godhead, Kṛṣṇa, having spoken thus to Arjuna, displayed His real four-armed form and at last showed His two-armed form, thus encouraging the fearful Arjuna.

The transition to Pratipadā at 1:36 PM marks the precise moment of cosmic renewal. The transition to Maghā — the royal star, the throne of the Pitṛs (ancestors) — marks the assumption of a new authority after the shedding. From serpent to king. From dissolution to sovereignty.

Karaṇa: Naga → Kimstughna. The Naga karaṇa (serpent) echoes the Āśleṣā nakṣatra's serpent energy — the final shedding. Kimstughna — the last karaṇa of the waning cycle, the threshold itself.

Special Observance: Hariyali Amāvasyā — the green new moon festival. In the Vedic tradition, this Amāvasyā celebrates the renewal of greenery during the monsoon season. It is a day for planting seeds — both literal and spiritual. The cosmic irony is precise: on the darkest day of the month, the seeds of renewal are sown.

Verse Selection Rationale

The selection of BG 11.50 for this Amāvasyā day is architecturally precise. Chapter 11's narrative arc reaches its resolution here: Arjuna has seen the terrifying universal form (viśva-rūpa), he has begged Kṛṣṇa to withdraw it, and now — in verse 50 — Kṛṣṇa restores His own form (*svakaṁ rūpam*). The terrifying, all-devouring cosmic form dissolves. In its place stands *saumya-vapuḥ* — the beautiful, gentle, two-armed form of Śyāmasundara.

This is the exact movement of Amāvasyā. The moon has fully waned — the old cycle is dissolved, the cosmic form withdrawn. What remains is the quiet, beautiful, essential form. The Pratipadā transition at 1:36 PM is the first waxing crescent — the return of the beautiful form, the beginning of a new cycle. The Āśleṣā → Maghā nakṣatra transition mirrors this at the stellar level: the serpent's coiled dissolution energy gives way to the royal throne of the ancestors. From shedding to sovereignty. From void to form.

As the final verse of the BG 11 cycle (Amāvasyā = last day of current chapter per rotation protocol), verse 50 provides the perfect capstone: the universal form that has been this chapter's theme is now withdrawn, and what remains is the personal, beautiful, accessible form of the Supreme — the form that even the devas eternally desire to see (11.52). This is the theological statement: after all cosmic revelation, what matters is the personal form. After dissolution, beauty.

Yoga Sūtra Connection: YS 1.3 — tada draṣṭuḥ svarūpe 'vasthānam

YS 1.3 states: tada draṣṭuḥ svarūpe 'vasthānam — "When that [cessation of mental modifications] is accomplished, the seer abides in its own true nature."

The parallel to BG 11.50 is structural and exact. Kṛṣṇa, having displayed the overwhelming universal form, now withdraws it and abides in His own form — *svakaṁ rūpam darśayām āsa*. This is the Divine demonstrating YS 1.3 from the cosmic perspective: after all modifications (the viśva-rūpa's infinite forms, all beings, all time, all destruction) are withdrawn, the Supreme Person abides in His own essential, beautiful nature (*saumya-vapuḥ*).

For the practitioner, the teaching is direct: the goal of yoga is not the acquisition of cosmic vision or power. The goal is to return to one's own true nature — *svarūpe avasthānam*. Arjuna's experience mirrors this. He is restored to his own nature in verse 11.51: *sa-cetāḥ prakṛtiṁ gataḥ* — "I am now composed in mind, restored to my original nature." The cosmic vision was necessary — it shattered his illusions — but the endpoint is not the vision. The endpoint is the return. The settling. The beautiful form.

The Sāṅkhya framework illuminates this further: prakṛti's modifications (the universal form's countless manifestations) are all prakṛti's play. When they subside, what remains is puruṣa — the pure witness, the seer abiding in its own form. Kṛṣṇa's *svakaṁ rūpam* is the spiritual analogue of puruṣa's *svarūpe avasthānam*. The cosmic form was prakṛti's full display; the beautiful form is puruṣa's quiet sovereignty.

Practical Application for the Day

On this Hariyali Amāvasyā, the practitioner stands at the threshold. The old cycle is gone — dissolved like the waning moon, shed like the serpent's skin of Āśleṣā. The new cycle has not yet fully begun — Pratipadā arrives at 1:36 PM, Maghā's royal star rises at 8:36 PM. This is the gap between. This is the space where practice matters most.

For the seeker: The Amāvasyā void is not emptiness — it is potential. The term *saumya-vapuḥ* (beautiful, gentle form) is the key. After confronting the overwhelming reality of the cosmic form — after seeing one's own karmic architecture, one's attachments, one's mortality — the response is not to remain in awe or terror. The response is to settle. To return to the beautiful form. To find the gentle essence that persists after all modification ceases. This is the practice of Amāvasyā: sit in the void, let the old dissolve, and discover what remains when everything else is stripped away.

For the practitioner: The transition from Āśleṣā to Maghā is the transition from serpent to throne. Āśleṣā's serpent energy is the karmic shedding — the uncomfortable, sometimes painful process of releasing what no longer serves. Maghā's royal energy is the assumption of authority — not ego, but the sovereignty that comes from having shed the false. The practice today is to allow the serpent's work to complete before claiming the throne. Do not rush into Pratipadā's new beginning. Let Amāvasyā's void do its work.

The Vajra Yoga of yesterday (11.18-19) was the unbreakable strength needed to face the cosmic form. Today's Variyan Yoga is the superior path — the road through the void, the path that is "best traversed" because it leads from dissolution to beauty. This is the path of *vairāgya* — not forceful renunciation but natural release, the gentle letting-go that reveals what was always there beneath the modifications.

As BG Chapter 11 closes, the teaching is complete: the Supreme can be terrifying in His fullness, but His essential nature is *saumya* — beautiful, gentle, accessible. The cosmic form is real, but it is not the goal. The goal is the beautiful form. The two-armed form. The form that even Brahmā and Śiva eternally desire to see. This is the Amāvasyā revelation: after the dark, the beautiful. After the void, the form. After the serpent's shedding, the king's throne.

Sanskrit Terms

svakaṁ rūpam (noun phrase) — His own form; the essential, original form of the Supreme, distinct from any manifested cosmic display. Like a system reverting to its clean default state after running a heavy diagnostic process.

saumya-vapuḥ (compound adjective) — beautiful, gentle form; the most beautiful form. *Saumya* derives from *soma* (the lunar essence, the nectar of tranquility). This is not just "pretty" — it is the form that calms the mind and restores the heart. The spiritual equivalent of returning to a clean, minimalist desktop after a kernel panic.

āśvāsayām āsa (verb) — encouraged, reassured, restored to calm; the act of dispelling fear. Kṛṣṇa does not just change form — He actively reassures. The Divine response to the devotee's terror is tenderness.

mahā-ātmā (compound noun) — the great-souled one; He whose self encompasses all selves. Not just "great" but the very definition of greatness as a quality of spirit.

Amāvasyā (noun) — the new moon; the day when the moon is not visible, the complete waning. From *ama* (together) + *vāsya* (dwelling) — the day when sun and moon dwell together in the same lunar mansion. The void is not empty — it is a convergence.

tada draṣṭuḥ svarūpe 'vasthānam (Yoga Sūra 1.3) — "then the seer abides in its own form"; the foundational statement of yoga's goal. *Svarūpe* (in its own form) and *avasthānam* (abiding) together describe the settled state — not striving, not becoming, simply being what one truly is.

Summary

BG 11.50 is the resolution of the cosmic vision — the Supreme withdraws the terrifying universal form and restores His beautiful, two-armed form (*saumya-vapuḥ*). On this Hariyali Amāvasyā, the day of void and renewal, this verse speaks directly to the practitioner's experience: after dissolution comes beauty. After the serpent's shedding (Āśleṣā) comes the king's throne (Maghā). After the waning of the old cycle comes the waxing of the new (Pratipadā at 1:36 PM).

As Chapter 11 closes — the chapter of cosmic vision, universal form, and the terrifying majesty of the Divine — the final teaching is not about power or awe. It is about return. *Svakaṁ rūpam* — His own form. *Saumya-vapuḥ* — the beautiful form. The form that the devas eternally long to see. The form that is the goal of all practice, all yoga, all dissolution: the beautiful, gentle, essential nature of the Supreme, and by reflection, the beautiful, gentle, essential nature of the self.
