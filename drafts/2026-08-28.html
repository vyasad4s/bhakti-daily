श्रीभगवानुवाच मय्यावेश्य मनो ये मां नित्ययुक्ता उपासते । श्रद्धया परयोपेतास्ते मे युक्ततमा मता: ॥ २ ॥
śrī-bhagavān uvāca
mayy āveśya mano ye māṁ
nitya-yuktā upāsate
śraddhayā parayopetās
te me yukta-tamā matāḥ
Translation (Prabhupada):
The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: Those who fix their minds on My personal form and are always engaged in worshiping Me with great and transcendental faith are considered by Me to be most perfect.

Today's celestial configuration carries the signature of new beginnings and warrior courage. Krishna Paksha Pratipada marks the first lunar day of the waning cycle — a time of release, but also of intentional departure. The waning moon is not passive; it is the active choice to let go. This is the martial energy of Ashwini (ruled by Mars), the celestial twin who guards new ventures and initiates movement where none existed before. When the Ashwini transfer to Bharani occurs mid-afternoon, the nakshatra shifts from pure speed and initiation to the deeper, more grounded energy of the "bright one" — Venus-ruled Bharani, the star of birth and creative power. One foot in sprint, one foot in fertile soil.

The day falls on Shukravar — Venus's day — but Venus here is not merely aesthetic. Venus in Jyotisha is Shukra, the deity who has drunk the nectar of immortality (amrita) and returned to teach others. He is the sage who has seen the other side and come back. This is the devotee's trajectory: not escapism, but return with wisdom. The planet that governs love, beauty, and creative force becomes today a vehicle for devotion rather than attachment.

Sukarman's yoga (good action) flowing into Dhruva (the fixed pole star) mirrors the practitioner's path: correct effort (karma-yoga) leading to steadiness of consciousness. The karaṇa cycle — Balava, then Kaulava, then Taitila — moves from strength (Bala = power) through sacred enjoyment (Kaulava) to a more stretched, deliberate quality. The day's arc invites the sadhaka to begin with vigor, find joy in the practice, and then sustain through discipline.

BG 12.2 answers Arjuna's question from 12.1 — "who is more perfect?" — with surgical clarity. It is not about methodology debates. It is about where you fix your attention. The personal form of the Lord is not a concession to sentiment; it is the direct object of meditation that delivers perfection. Fix the mind on the Form, keep it there, and everything becomes devotional activity.

Practice for Today:

Choose one daily activity — cooking, walking, bathing, or any routine task — and perform it with full, uninterrupted attention to Kṛṣṇa's presence. Do not add extra rounds of japa or reading. Simply take what you already do and make it continuous. This is mayy āveśya manaḥ in action: fix the mind on the Form and stay there. When the mind drifts (and it will), notice the drift and return to the task. That return is the practice. Ashwini's speed will test your steadiness; Bharani's fertility will reward your patience. No dramatic sadhana required — just ordinary actions made sacred by unbroken attention.

Yoga Sutra Connection:

BG 12.2's description of fixing the mind on the Divine (mayy āveśya manaḥ) finds its parallel in Yoga Sutra 1.1:

atha yoga-anuśāsanam — "Now, therefore, the instruction of yoga."

Patañjali opens not with a definition but with a now. The sūtra assumes the student is already here, already ready. Similarly, BG 12.2 assumes the practitioner is already engaged — nitya-yuktā, always engaged. This is not beginner's yoga; this is the mature sadhaka who has already committed. The sutra's "now" is the cosmic moment of Ashwini — new, immediate, demanding response. The cessation of mental fluctuations (citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ, YS 1.2) that follows is not a technique; it is the natural result of fixing attention on the Form so completely that nothing else can take hold.

From a Sankhya perspective: buddhi (discriminating intelligence) is the faculty that performs mayy āveśya. When buddhi is directed toward puruṣa (the Lord as the supreme knower), it performs its proper function. When it is misdirected toward prakṛti (the body, the world of objects), it produces confusion. BG 12.2 gives buddhi its correct object — the personal form — and in doing so, establishes the foundation for samādhi.

Sanskrit Terms:

Mayy āveśya (मय्यावेश्य) — fixing within Me; The mind's intentional relocation from the world of objects to the form of the Lord. Ā-veśa is not possession; it is attention raised to the level of absorption.

Śraddhayā parayā (श्रद्धया परया) — with supreme faith; Not blind belief but a quality of certainty that arises from direct experience of the sacred. Para means beyond — this faith points toward the transcendent.

Yukta-tamāḥ (युक्ततमा) — most perfect in yoga; The superlative of yukta, meaning "united" or "engaged." These practitioners are not partially united — they are most perfectly so.
