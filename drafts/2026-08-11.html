Bhagavad-gītā 11.29

yathā pradīptaṁ jvalanaṁ pataṅgā
viśanti nāśāya samṛddha-vegāḥ
tathaiva nāśāya viśanti lokās
tavāpi vaktrāṇi samṛddha-vegāḥ

yathā pradīptaṁ jvalanaṁ pataṅgā — as a blazing fire attracts moths; viśanti nāśāya samṛddha-vegāḥ — they enter for destruction with full speed; tathaiva nāśāya viśanti lokās — similarly, all people enter for destruction; tavāpi vaktrāṇi samṛddha-vegāḥ — into Your mouths with full speed.

I see all people rushing full speed into Your mouths, as moths dash to destruction in a blazing fire.

Verse Selection Rationale

The selection of BG 11.29 for this Caturdaśī day is surgically precise. The verse describes the inevitable rushing of all beings into the Divine's flaming mouths — moths drawn to destruction in a blazing fire. This is the exact movement of Vyatipāta Yoga: the falling away, the severing, the final dissolution that precedes renewal.

The Puṣya → Āśleṣā nakṣatra transition provides the perfect cosmic backdrop: Puṣya, the guru star (Bṛhaspati), offers its last nourishment and guidance before Āśleṣā's serpent energy takes over — the karmic shedding that strips away illusion. The verse's "moths dash to destruction" mirrors Āśleṣā's viśa-śakti (poison-power) that dissolves what no longer serves. The guru's final word before the serpent's work.

Caturdaśī — the fourteenth day — is the last complete step before the void. In the 15-day pakṣa cycle, this is the final full day of light before the darkness of Amāvasyā. The verse captures this threshold moment: not yet dissolved, but inexorably drawn toward the flames. All people (all beings, all tendencies, all karmic patterns) are rushing full speed into the mouths — not in terror, but in the inevitable pull of dissolution that precedes rebirth.

As the penultimate verse of the BG 11 cycle (with Amāvasyā = last day of current chapter per rotation protocol), verse 29 provides the perfect prelude to tomorrow's capstone (BG 11.50): today we see the rushing into the flames; tomorrow we see the withdrawal of the cosmic form and the return to the beautiful form. The falling away leads to the settling.

Yoga Sūtra Connection: YS 2.2 — samādhi-bhāvanārthaḥ kleśa-tanū-karaṇārthaś ca

YS 2.2 states: samādhi-bhāvanārthaḥ kleśa-tanū-karaṇārthaś ca — "For the purpose of cultivating samādhi and for the purpose of diminishing the kleśas."

The direct parallel to BG 11.29 is exact and mechanical. The "rushing full speed into Your mouths" is the diminishment of kleśas (impurities, attachments, ego-identifications). The "moths dash to destruction in a blazing fire" is the unavoidable pull of dissolution that burns away what obscures the true nature. This process — the diminishing of kleśas — serves the dual purpose stated in YS 2.2:

1. For the purpose of cultivating samādhi: As the kleśas fall away, what remains is the puruṣa — the pure witness — abiding in its own nature (YS 1.3). The dissolution is not an end but a preparation: the clearing of the field for the seed of union.

2. For the purpose of diminishing the kleśas: The blazing fire of the Divine's mouths is the very instrument of kleśa-tanū-karaṇa — the thinning (tanū) of the kleśas until they can no longer obstruct the light. Just as moths are consumed by the flame, so too are our impurities consumed by the Divine's gaze.

The verse shows the mechanism; the sutra reveals the purpose. Together they form the complete teaching: the rushing into the flames is not annihilation but purification — the necessary thinning of kleśas that allows samādhi to dawn.

Practical Application for the Day

On this Caturdaśī Vyatipāta day, the practitioner stands at the gates of dissolution. The old cycle is nearly complete — only the final threshold remains. The energy is one of urgent completion: finish what must be finished, release what must be released, before entering the void.

For the seeker: The moths-and-flame metaphor is the key. Do not resist the pull. Do not delay the surrender. The rushing into the mouths is not metaphorical — it is the actual process of kleśa-dissolution that occurs when one ceases resistance. The practice today is to allow the rushing: to let the attachments, the identifications, the old patterns move with full speed toward their dissolution in the Divine's gaze. This is not self-annihilation but self-forgetting — the loss of the false to reveal the true.

For the practitioner: The Puṣya → Āśleṣā transition is the transition from guru's guidance to serpent's shedding. Puṣya's energy (Bṛhaspati, the teacher) offers the final discriminative wisdom: discern what is eternal from what is temporary. Then Āśleṣā takes over — the serpent strips away what the guru has illuminated as false. The practice is to heed the guru's last teachings while allowing the serpent to do its work. Do not cling to the guidance; do not obstruct the shedding.

Vyatipāta Yoga — "the falling away" — is the superior path for days like today. This is not gentle release but decisive severance. The bonds that must fall are not loose threads but thick ropes of identification: I am this body, I am this mind, I am this karma, I am this reputation. The practice is to invite the severance: to ask, with full sincerity, "What must fall away today?" and then to not obstruct the answer when it comes.

As BG Chapter 11 progresses toward its conclusion, the teaching deepens: the cosmic form is terrifying not because it destroys but because it reveals — it reveals what has always been true: that all things return to their source. The moths do not choose the flame; they are drawn by their nature. So too are we drawn to dissolve what is not eternal. The practice of Caturdaśī is to align with this nature: to stop resisting the pull, to stop delaying the surrender, and to allow the full-speed rushing into the mouths that purifies rather than destroys.

Tomorrow's Amāvasyā will show the withdrawn cosmic form and the return of saumya-vapuḥ. Today's practice is to make that return possible: to thin the kleśas, to sever the bonds, to allow the full-speed rushing that prepares the ground for the beautiful form to dawn.

Sanskrit Terms

pradīptaṁ jvalanaṁ (compound) — blazing fire; fire that is fully ignited, radiating intense heat and light. The spiritual equivalent of a system at maximum diagnostic load, ready to burn away corruption.

pataṅgā (noun) — moth; the insect drawn irresistibly to flame, symbolizing the jīva's inevitable attraction to destruction of illusion. Not weakness but nature — the pull is built into the design.

viśanti nāśāya (verb phrase) — they enter for destruction; the purposeful movement toward dissolution. Not accidental but intentional — the soul's nature seeks liberation through the destruction of what binds.

samṛddha-vegāḥ (compound adjective) — with full speed; complete velocity, maximum momentum. Not gradual but decisive — the kleśas fall away not in dribs and drabs but in a rush, like water breaking through a dam.

vaktrāṇi (noun plural) — mouths; the openings of the cosmic form, the points of dissolution and absorption. Like the input ports of a system designed to consume and transform.

samādhi-bhāvanārthaḥ kleśa-tanū-karaṇārthaś ca (Yoga Sūtra 2.2) — "For the purpose of cultivating samādhi and for the purpose of diminishing the kleśas"; the dual purpose of spiritual practice: to cultivate union and to thin the impurities that obstruct it.
