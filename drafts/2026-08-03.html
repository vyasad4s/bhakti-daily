Bhagavad Gita Chapter 12, Verse 2

मय्यावेश्य मनो ये मां नित्ययुक्ता उपासते । श्रद्धया परयोपेतास्ते मे युक्ततमा मता: ॥ २ ॥

 

mayy āveśya mano ye māṁ
nitya-yuktā upāsate
śraddhayā parayopetās
te me yukta-tamā matāḥ

 

The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: Those who fix their minds on My personal form and are always engaged in worshiping Me with great and transcendental faith are considered by Me to be most perfect.

 

Today is Somavāra (Monday), ruled by the Moon — the presiding deity of the mind, emotions, and reflection. The Moon currently resides in Meena (Pisces), the mutable-water sign of compassion, dissolution, and unity. The tithi is Kṛṣṇa Pakṣa Pañcamī (stabilization) transitioning to Ṣaṣṭhī (effort) at 1:24 PM — a juncture where the gains of obstacle-removal (Caturthī) are consolidated and directed toward purposeful action. The nakṣatra is Uttara Bhādrapadā (until 12:30 PM), governed by Ahir Budhnya (serpent of depths — stability, deep waters), shifting to Revatī (after 12:30 PM), governed by Pūṣan (the nourisher, protector, and guide of souls on their journey). The yoga is Sukarman (auspicious action, well-doing) until 11:43 AM, transitioning to Dhṛti (steadiness, resolve, holding firm) thereafter. These energies align precisely with BG 12.2: the Moon (mind) fixed on Kṛṣṇa’s personal form (mayy āveśya mano), with unwavering engagement (nitya-yuktā — echoed by Dhṛti yoga), and transcendental faith (śraddhayā parayopetās — the nourishing, guiding grace of Pūṣan/Revatī leading to the supreme goal: being deemed yukta-tamā, most perfect in union, by Kṛṣṇa Himself).

 

BG 12.2 is Kṛṣṇa’s direct answer to Arjuna’s question in BG 12.1 about which devotees are most perfect: those who worship the personal form with devotion or those who meditate on the impersonal Brahman. Kṛṣṇa declares that the personalist — the one whose mind is fixed on His form (mayy āveśya mano), constantly engaged in His service (nitya-yuktā upāsate), and endowed with śraddhayā parayopetā (faith that is transcendental, not blind belief but conviction born of realization) — is yukta-tamā, “most perfect in yoga.” This is not a vague compliment; it is a technical statement from the Supreme Yogeśvara about the state of consciousness that constitutes perfection in the yogic path. The verse contains the entire method: fix the mind (āsana, pratyāhāra, dhāraṇa), engage in service (karma-yoga transformed into bhakti), and cultivate faith that is śraddhā — not gullibility, but the steadfast trust that arises when the intellect perceives the truth of the personal Divine. Today’s Revatī nakṣatra, governed by Pūṣan (the deity who nourishes, protects, and guides souls on their journey), provides the perfect cosmic backdrop: just as Pūṣan guides and nourishes the traveler, Kṛṣṇa guides and nourishes the devotee whose mind is fixed on Him. Dhṛti yoga (steadiness, resolve) mirrors nitya-yuktā — the unwavering, constant engagement. The Pañcamī→Ṣaṣṭhī tithi transition (stabilization→effort) reflects the two halves of the verse: fixing the mind (stabilization of consciousness) and constant engagement (directed effort in service). The Moon in Meena adds the flavor of dissolution into unity — the blurring of the false boundary between devotee and Devotee that occurs when the mind is truly fixed.

 

From a Sāṅkhya perspective, BG 12.2 describes the state where puruṣa (conscious principle) has disengaged from misidentification with prakṛti (matter/mind-modifications) and established its reference point in the personal Divine. “Fixing the mind on My personal form” (mayy āveśya mano) is not concentration on an imagined form; it is the redirection of citta (mind-stuff) from its habitual modifications (vṛttis) — sense gratification, speculation, egoic identifications — toward the awareness of the personal Supreme. This is viveka (discrimination) in action: discerning puruṣa from prakṛti and aligning buddhi (intellect) with puruṣa’s true nature and refuge. “Always engaged in worshiping Me” (nitya-yuktā upāsate) is the sustained engagement of puruṣa in its natural activity — loving service — rather than allowing prakṛti to drive the senses and mind toward fleeting objects. “With great and transcendental faith” (śraddhayā parayopetās) is the buddhi’s conviction, born of discrimination and experience, that the personal Divine is the highest truth — not a belief imposed from outside, but the recognition of what is already real when the mind is purified. The result? Being considered yukta-tamā by Śrī Kṛṣṇa Himself — not a subjective label, but the objective state of consciousness where yoga (union) is most perfect. Today’s Dhṛti yoga (the power to hold steady) and Revatī’s Pūṣan (the guide who ensures the traveler reaches the destination) are the cosmic confirmations: the path is steady, the guide is present, and the goal is union with the Personal.

 

YOGA SUTRA CONNECTION

YS 1.2: yogaḥ citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ — “Yoga is the cessation of the modifications of the mind.” BG 12.2 is the positive expression of this sutra: when the mind is fixed on the personal Divine (mayy āveśya mano) and constantly engaged in His service (nitya-yuktā), the modifications (vṛttis) naturally cease — not through force, but through absorption in the higher object. The cessation is not the goal; it is the byproduct of the mind’s genuine engagement with the personal Supreme.

YS 1.23: īśvara praṇidhānād vā — “Or, [this state is attainable] from devotion to the Lord.” BG 12.2 details what īśvara-praṇidhāna looks like: fixing the mind on the personal form (not the impersonal brahma-jyotir), constant engagement (nitya-yuktā), and transcendental faith (śraddhayā parayopetā). It is not vague devotion; it is specific, measurable, and personal.

YS 2.1: tapas svādhyāya īśvara-praṇidhānā kriyā-yogaḥ — The threefold practice of Kriya Yoga. BG 12.2 contains all three: tapas (the discipline of nitya-yuktā — constant engagement despite distractions), svādhyāya (the study of His nature through constant remembrance and service), and īśvara-praṇidhāna (fixing the mind on the personal Divine). The verse shows that when these three are practiced, the result is being deemed yukta-tamā — most perfect in union.

YS I.12: abhyāsa vairāgyābhyāṁ tannirodhaḥ — “The cessation of mental modifications is achieved through consistent practice and detachment.” BG 12.2’s nitya-yuktā is abhyāsa (consistent practice of fixing the mind and engaging in service). Its implied detachment is vairāgya — freedom from the modifying tendencies of prakṛti that would pull the mind away from the personal Divine. The Moon in Meena (dissolution, unity) supports this vairāgya: the sense of separateness dissolves when the mind is fixed on the personal.

 

PRACTICE FOR TODAY

Fixing the Mind (Uttara Bhādrapadā Morning): Until 12:30 PM, Uttara Bhādrapadā is active — the stability nakṣatra governed by Ahir Budhnya (serpent of depths). Before japa, sit quietly and observe: where is your mind drifting? Is it fixed on tasks, anxieties, plans, or memories? Gently, without force, bring your attention back to the personal form of Kṛṣṇa — not as an abstraction, but as the Supreme Person who is the source of all. Let the steadiness of Ahir Budhnya support this fixing: like the serpent holding deep in the waters, let your attention remain steady, undisturbed by surface fluctuations.

Transcendental Faith (Revatī Afternoon): After 12:30 PM, Revatī governs — Pūṣan, the nourisher and guide. During this period, consciously cultivate śraddhayā parayopetā — faith that is not blind, but nourished by reflection and experience. Recall moments when you felt Kṛṣṇa’s guidance, protection, or nourishment — not as fantasy, but as recognition of His presence. Let this recollection strengthen your conviction: “He is real. He is personal. He is my refuge.” This is not emotional excitement; it is the steady nourishment of the intellect, like Pūṣan providing sustenance to the traveler on the path.

Constant Engagement (Dhṛti Yoga & Soma Vāra): Dhṛti yoga (steadiness, resolve) is active after 11:43 AM, and Somavāra (Monday) emphasizes the mind’s connection to the Moon. Use this combination to practice nitya-yuktā — constant, unwavering engagement. Select one routine activity (washing dishes, answering emails, driving) and perform it with the sankalpa: “This is for You.” Not as an occasional offering, but as a steady stream: let each action be an expression of mat-karma-kṛt (action done for Him). Let the steadiness of Dhṛti and the lunar influence of Somavāra keep your engagement unbroken, like a steady flame protected from wind.

 

TERM DEFINITIONS:

mayy āveśya mano (compound) — “fixing the mind on Me”; mat (My) + āveśya (fixing, placing) + manaḥ (mind). Not imagination, but the redirection of attention from mental modifications to the awareness of the personal Divine.

nitya-yuktā (compound) — “always engaged”; nitya (always, constant) + yuktā (engaged, united). Steady, uninterrupted engagement in devotional service — not sporadic, but a constant stream of consciousness.

upāsate (verb) — “worship, serve, attend to.” From upa (near) + ās (to sit, dwell). To dwell near the Divine through service — cooking, cleaning, speaking, working — done with the consciousness of proximity to the Personal.

śraddhayā (noun) — “with faith”; śraddhā (faith, conviction, trust). Not blind belief, but the steady trust that arises when the intellect perceives truth. Parayopetā modifies it: “endowed with transcendental faith” — faith that surpasses ordinary belief and is rooted in realization.

yukta-tamā (compound) — “most perfect in union, most perfect in yoga”; yukta (united, joined, engaged) + tamā (superlative suffix). The state where yoga (union) is most perfect — not a title, but an objective description of consciousness where the mind is fixed, engagement is constant, and faith is transcendental.

uttara bhadrapada (noun, nakṣatra) — “the latter good foot.” Ruled by Ahir Budhnya, the serpent of the depths. Governs stability, deep waters, patience, and the unshakable foundation.

ahir budhnya (proper noun, deity) — “the serpent of the deep.” The deity of Uttara Bhādrapadā nakṣatra, representing the primal, stabilizing force in the cosmic waters.

revati (noun, nakṣatra) — “the wealthy one.” Ruled by Pūṣan, the nourisher, protector, and guide. Governs nourishment, protection, guidance, and the successful completion of journeys.

pūṣan (proper noun, deity) — “the nourisher.” The deity of Revatī nakṣatra, who guides and protects souls on their journey, ensuring they reach their destination.

dhṛti (noun, yoga) — “steadiness, resolve, holding firm.” One of the 27 yogas, representing the power to maintain focus, remain steady under pressure, and hold to one’s purpose.

sukarman (noun, yoga) — “well-doing, auspicious action.” One of the 27 yogas, representing actions that are naturally aligned with dharma and produce positive results.

pañcamī (noun, tithi) — “the fifth.” The tithi following Caturthī, representing stabilization, consolidation, and grounding.

ṣaṣṭhī (noun, tithi) — “the sixth.” The tithi following Pañcamī, representing effort, endeavor, and directed action toward a goal.

somavāra (noun, vāra) — “Monday.” Ruled by the Moon (Chandra), presiding deity of the mind, emotions, reflection, and the inner world.

meena (noun, rāśi) — “Pisces.” The mutable-water sign of dissolution, compassion, and the return to the source. Governs the vision of unity, the breaking down of barriers between self and other.

karka (noun, rāśi) — “Cancer.” The cardinal-water sign of nurturing, protection, and the maternal principle. Governs the home, the heart, and the foundation of emotional security.

mat-karma-kṛt (compound) — “one who engages in My activities”; mat (My) + karma (activities, work) + kṛt (doer/engager). Not karma as bondage, but every action offered as service — cooking, cleaning, working, speaking — done with the consciousness: “This is for You.”

mat-paramaḥ (compound) — “whose supreme goal is Me”; mat (My) + paramaḥ (highest, supreme goal). The intellect’s highest reference point is Kṛṣṇa; all other pursuits are secondary.

mad-bhaktaḥ (compound) — “My devotee”; mat (My) + bhaktaḥ (devotee, one who possesses bhakti). Bhakti is not emotion; it is the sustained orientation of the heart toward the Supreme Person as the object of love and service.

saṅga-varjitaḥ (compound) — “free from association”; saṅga (association, connection) + varjitaḥ (given up, avoided). Not isolation, but freedom from contaminating motives: association with sense gratification, speculative philosophy, or fruitive desires that distract from bhakti.

nirvairaḥ (adjective) — “devoid of envy, free from hostility”; nir (without) + vairaḥ (envy, hostility, rivalry). The natural fruit of seeing Kṛṣṇa everywhere: no jealousy toward others’ blessings, no competition for limited resources, no ill-will — because all beings are seen as parts of the same whole.

citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ (compound) — “cessation of the modifications of the mind”; citta (mind-stuff) + vṛtti (modifications) + nirodhaḥ (cessation). The definition of yoga in YS 1.2: not suppression, but the natural stilling of mental modifications when the mind is absorbed in a higher object.

abhyāsa (noun) — “consistent practice, repetition.” The steady, repeated effort to fix the mind and engage in service.

vairāgya (noun) — “detachment, dispassion.” Freedom from the modifying tendencies of prakṛti that would pull the mind away from the personal Divine.

kriyā-yogaḥ (compound) — “the yoga of action”; kriyā (action) + yoga (union). The threefold practice: tapas (discipline), svādhyāya (self-study), īśvara-praṇidhāna (devotion to the Lord).
