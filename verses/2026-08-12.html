🏹 BG 11.50 | 2026-08-12

 

sañjaya uvāca

ity arjunaṁ vāsudevas tathoktvā

svakaṁ rūpaṁ darśayām āsa bhūyaḥ

āśvāsayām āsa ca bhītam enaṁ

bhūtvā punaḥ saumya-vapur mahātmā

 

sañjaya uvāca — Sañjaya said; ity arjunaṁ vāsudevaḥ tathā uktvā — having thus spoken to Arjuna, Vāsudeva; svakam rūpam darśayām āsa bhūyaḥ — showed His own form again; āśvāsayām āsa ca bhītam enam — and encouraged the fearful Arjuna; bhūtvā punaḥ — becoming again; saumya-vapuḥ — the beautiful form; mahā-ātmā — the great one.

 

Sañjaya said to Dhṛtarāṣṭra: The Supreme Personality of Godhead, Kṛṣṇa, having spoken thus to Arjuna, displayed His real four-armed form and at last showed His two-armed form, thus encouraging the fearful Arjuna.

 

Amāvasyā Capstone — Return to the Beautiful Form — Chapter 11's narrative arc reaches its resolution. Arjuna has seen the terrifying universal form (viśva-rūpa), he has begged Kṛṣṇa to withdraw it, and now Kṛṣṇa restores His own form (svakaṁ rūpam). The terrifying, all-devouring cosmic form dissolves. In its place stands saumya-vapuḥ — the beautiful, gentle, two-armed form of Śyāmasundara. This is the exact movement of Amāvasyā: the moon has fully waned, the old cycle dissolved, and what remains is the quiet, beautiful, essential form.

The term saumya-vapuḥ is critical. Saumya derives from soma — the lunar essence, the nectar of tranquility. This is not just "pretty" — it is the form that calms the mind and restores the heart. Kṛṣṇa does not just change form; He actively reassures (āśvāsayām āsa). The Divine response to the devotee's terror is tenderness. The word mahā-ātmā — the great-souled one — confirms that this beautiful form is not a diminishment of the cosmic vision but its highest expression. The greatest soul shows the gentlest face.

 

🔥 YS 1.23 — Īśvara-Praṇidhāna Connection: YS 1.3 states: tada draṣṭuḥ svarūpe 'vasthānam — "When that [cessation of mental modifications] is accomplished, the seer abides in its own true nature." The parallel to BG 11.50 is structural and exact. Kṛṣṇa withdraws the overwhelming universal form and abides in His own form — svakaṁ rūpam darśayām āsa. This is the Divine demonstrating YS 1.3 from the cosmic perspective: after all modifications (the viśva-rūpa's infinite forms, all beings, all time, all destruction) are withdrawn, the Supreme Person abides in His own essential, beautiful nature (saumya-vapuḥ).

For the practitioner, the teaching is direct: the goal of yoga is not the acquisition of cosmic vision or power. The goal is to return to one's own true nature — svarūpe avasthānam. Arjuna's experience mirrors this. He is restored to his own nature in verse 11.51: sa-cetāḥ prakṛtiṁ gataḥ — "I am now composed in mind, restored to my original nature." The cosmic vision was necessary — it shattered his illusions — but the endpoint is not the vision. The endpoint is the return. The settling. The beautiful form.

The Sāṅkhya framework illuminates this further: prakṛti's modifications (the universal form's countless manifestations) are all prakṛti's play. When they subside, what remains is puruṣa — the pure witness, the seer abiding in its own form. Kṛṣṇa's svakaṁ rūpam is the spiritual analogue of puruṣa's svarūpe avasthānam. The cosmic form was prakṛti's full display; the beautiful form is puruṣa's quiet sovereignty.

 

Practical Application for the Day: On this Hariyali Amāvasyā, the practitioner stands at the threshold. The old cycle is gone — dissolved like the waning moon, shed like the serpent's skin of Āśleṣā. The new cycle has not yet fully begun — Pratipadā arrives at 1:36 PM, Maghā's royal star rises at 8:36 PM. This is the gap between. This is the space where practice matters most.

The Amāvasyā void is not emptiness — it is potential. After confronting the overwhelming reality of the cosmic form — after seeing one's own karmic architecture, one's attachments, one's mortality — the response is not to remain in awe or terror. The response is to settle. To return to the beautiful form. To find the gentle essence that persists after all modification ceases. This is the practice of Amāvasyā: sit in the void, let the old dissolve, and discover what remains when everything else is stripped away.

The transition from Āśleṣā to Maghā is the transition from serpent to throne. Āśleṣā's serpent energy is the karmic shedding — the uncomfortable, sometimes painful process of releasing what no longer serves. Maghā's royal energy is the assumption of authority — not ego, but the sovereignty that comes from having shed the false. The practice today is to allow the serpent's work to complete before claiming the throne. Do not rush into Pratipadā's new beginning. Let Amāvasyā's void do its work.

As BG Chapter 11 closes, the teaching is complete: the Supreme can be terrifying in His fullness, but His essential nature is saumya — beautiful, gentle, accessible. The cosmic form is real, but it is not the goal. The goal is the beautiful form. The two-armed form. The form that even Brahmā and Śiva eternally desire to see. This is the Amāvasyā revelation: after the dark, the beautiful. After the void, the form. After the serpent's shedding, the king's throne.

 

TERM DEFINITIONS:

svakaṁ rūpam (noun phrase) — His own form; the essential, original form of the Supreme, distinct from any manifested cosmic display. Like a system reverting to its clean default state after running a heavy diagnostic process.

saumya-vapuḥ (compound adjective) — beautiful, gentle form; the most beautiful form. Saumya derives from soma (the lunar essence, the nectar of tranquility). This is not just "pretty" — it is the form that calms the mind and restores the heart. The spiritual equivalent of returning to a clean, minimalist desktop after a kernel panic.

āśvāsayām āsa (verb) — encouraged, reassured, restored to calm; the act of dispelling fear. Kṛṣṇa does not just change form — He actively reassures. The Divine response to the devotee's terror is tenderness.

mahā-ātmā (compound noun) — the great-souled one; He whose self encompasses all selves. Not just "great" but the very definition of greatness as a quality of spirit.

amāvasyā (noun) — the new moon; the day when the moon is not visible, the complete waning. From ama (together) + vāsya (dwelling) — the day when sun and moon dwell together in the same lunar mansion. The void is not empty — it is a convergence.

tada draṣṭuḥ svarūpe 'vasthānam (Yoga Sūtra 1.3) — "then the seer abides in its own form"; the foundational statement of yoga's goal. Svarūpe (in its own form) and avasthānam (abiding) together describe the settled state — not striving, not becoming, simply being what one truly is.
