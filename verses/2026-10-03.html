🏹 BG 15.3–4 | 2026-10-03

 

न रूपमस्येह तथोपलभ्यते

नान्तो न चादिर्न च सम्प्रतिष्ठा ।

अश्वत्थमेनं सुविरूढमूल-

मसङ्गशस्त्रेण दृढेन छित्त्वा ॥ ३ ॥

तत: पदं तत्परिमार्गितव्यं

यस्मिन्गता न निवर्तन्ति भूय: ।

तमेव चाद्यं पुरुषं प्रपद्ये

यत: प्रवृत्ति: प्रसृता पुराणी ॥ ४ ॥

 

na rūpam asyeha tathopalabhyate
nānto na cādir na ca sampratiṣṭhā
aśvattham enaṁ su-virūḍha-mūlam
asaṅga-śastreṇa dṛḍhena chittvā
tataḥ padaṁ tat parimārgitavyaṁ
yasmin gatā na nivartanti bhūyaḥ
tam eva cādyaṁ puruṣaṁ prapadye
yataḥ pravṛttiḥ prasṛtā purāṇī

 

The real form of this tree cannot be perceived in this world. No one can understand where it ends, where it begins, or where its foundation is. But with determination one must cut down this strongly rooted tree with the weapon of detachment. Thereafter, one must seek that place from which, having gone, one never returns, and there surrender to that Supreme Personality of Godhead from whom everything began and from whom everything has extended since time immemorial.

 

Look at what this verse admits: the tree has no perceivable form, no visible end, no locatable beginning, no discoverable foundation. Saṁsāra is a system you are embedded in — and from inside a system, you cannot map its boundaries. Every vantage point you could occupy is itself a branch. The mind that tries to see the whole tree is made of the tree's substance, running the tree's processes. This is why no amount of introspection from within the banyan ever yields the banyan's architecture. The data is everywhere; the overview is nowhere. So Krishna does not prescribe study. He prescribes severance. asaṅga-śastreṇa dṛḍhena chittvā — cut with the strong weapon of non-attachment. Note the precision here: attachment is not a symptom of entanglement, it is the load-bearing structure. The tree of saṁsāra is not held together by wood and bark — it is held together by your investment in it. Every branch you have ever climbed, you climbed because something in you said "this is mine, this is me, this is what I want." Cut that root-sentence and the entire architecture loses its support. This is vairāgya from first principles: not a mood of indifference, not renunciation-as-aesthetic, but the surgical destruction of the claim that prakṛti's outputs belong to you. The order of operations matters. First chittvā — having cut. Then parimārgitavyam — the search begins. The place of no-return cannot be found while you are still gripping the tree. Detachment is not the goal of the spiritual life; it is the precondition for accurate perception. A mind still tethered to a branch cannot trace the origin of the branching. Only the severed observer sees the root-system for what it is — and sees past it, to the puruṣa from whom the pravṛtti, the original outflow, first extended. And then the verse does something remarkable: it ends in first person. prapadye — "I surrender." The instruction collapses into demonstration. The map to the exit is not a set of directions but a voice saying "this is where I placed my weight." Surrender here is not emotional collapse; it is the final act of precision — returning consciousness to its source-point, the original Person from whom everything has been streaming since before memory. The verse's own structure is the practice: perceive the un-mappability, cut the attachment, search the source, surrender.

 

🔥 YOGA SUTRA CONNECTION: YS I.15: dṛṣṭānuśravikaviṣayavitṛṣṇasya vaśīkārasaṁjñā vairāgyam — "The mastery known as vairāgya is the freedom from thirst for objects seen and heard."
YS I.12: abhyāsa-vairāgyābhyāṁ tan-nirodhaḥ — "The fluctuations of mind are restrained by practice and dispassion together."
The Gītā calls the weapon asaṅga; Patañjali names it vairāgya — and defines it not as a feeling but as vaśīkāra, mastery. This is the shared technical insight of both texts: detachment is not suppression, not numbing, not a performance of indifference. It is the deliberate withdrawal of thirst (vitṛṣṇā) from every object the senses have seen or merely heard about — including the imagined pleasures the mind projects onto branches it has never climbed. YS I.12 pairs vairāgya with abhyāsa, practice — which is the Gītā's dṛḍhena, the firmness of grip on the blade. One does not cut a tree this old with a loose hand. Both systems agree on the mechanism: the vṛttis — the mind's cycling through the tree's branches — are not destroyed by force but starved by the removal of investment. When thirst goes, the tree's grip goes with it. Nirodha is what remains when the mind stops watering its own captivity.

 

TERM DEFINITIONS:

asaṅga (adjective/noun) — non-attachment; the weapon of the verse. Etymologically "without-saṅga" — saṅga is contact, linkage, dependency. Like removing every import a process depends on: the process dies not by attack, but by severed linkage.

śastra (noun) — weapon; listen for the near-twin śāstra (scripture): in this verse they collapse — the cutting implement IS knowledge.

dṛḍhena (adverb) — firmly, with resolve; not a tentative edit — a decisive operation applied with full grip.

chittvā (gerund) — having cut; the verb precedes everything else in the sequence; no search is possible before the severance completes.

parimārgitavyam (obligational gerundive) — must be thoroughly sought out; an exhaustive trace — full-stack debug — not back through the branches, but forward to the origin point.

padaṁ (noun) — position, abode, station; the root directory that was never a branch.

pravṛttiḥ (noun) — the outflow, the first extension; the initial process spawned at boot — everything in the material tree descends from that first fork.

purāṇī (adjective) — ancient, from before memory; legacy code running since before you had credentials — but there was a first commit, and it has an author.

Evening Application

Kalashtami gives you one instruction: find your grip and release it once, deliberately. Before the Moon crosses into Punarvasu at 3:59 PM, name the single attachment that most reliably pulls you back into the tree — the one habit, resentment, or craving you have been negotiating with instead of cutting. You do not need to sever everything today. You need one clean cut, made firmly (dṛḍhena), to remember what the blade feels like. Then in the evening, as Navami begins and Punarvasu's light returns, sit for ten minutes and trace in reverse: this impulse arose from a thought, the thought from an old pattern, the pattern from an older one — back toward the first extension (pravṛtti). You will not reach the root tonight. You are not required to. You are required only to stop defending the branch you are standing on. The place of no-return is not far; it is simply unmapped from inside the tree. Cut once, and the search has already begun.
